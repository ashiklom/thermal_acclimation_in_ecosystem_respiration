Thermal acclimation of ecosystem respiration

How strongly does ecosystem respiration adjust to warmer-than-usual temperatures? Estimates for eddy-covariance flux tower sites across the world's biomes.

Plants, microbes and soil animals release CO₂ when they respire, and in the short term this ecosystem respiration rises with temperature. Over weeks to seasons, though, organisms can acclimate: after a warm spell their respiration may be lower than the same temperature would have produced in a cooler year (down-regulation), or higher (enhancement). Which of the two prevails, and by how much, decides whether warming speeds up the loss of ecosystem carbon to the atmosphere more or less than short-term temperature responses suggest.

Flux towers measure the CO₂ exchange between an ecosystem and the atmosphere every half hour, for many years, together with air and soil temperature, soil moisture and sunlight. At night there is no photosynthesis, so the measured CO₂ flux is ecosystem respiration. For each site, each two-week window of the growing season and each year, a respiration–temperature curve is fitted to the high-quality nighttime records; from it comes the respiration expected at that window's typical (reference) conditions. Comparing this reference respiration across years — warmer years against cooler years — measures acclimation:

  • Thermal acclimation sensitivity (TAS) is the change in the logarithm of reference respiration per °C by which a window was warmer than in other years. TAS = −0.02 means that respiration at the same reference temperature is about 2 % lower for every 1 °C of warmer conditions (acclimation that dampens the warming response); a positive TAS means it is higher (an amplifying feedback).
  • Total TAS uses a respiration model with temperature only, so it includes every way a warmer period changes respiration: the physiological adjustment itself, and indirect effects through the soil moisture and the supply of fresh photosynthate (substrate) that change along with temperature.
  • Direct TAS uses a model that also accounts for soil moisture and for the day's photosynthetic uptake (daytime net CO₂ exchange). What remains is the direct effect of temperature history on respiration.

Respiration responds to the temperature where it happens, mostly in the soil, so the estimates use soil temperature by default; where a site's soil-temperature sensor is missing or unreliable, it is reconstructed or predicted from air temperature (see Soil temperature in detail). Every estimate is also available with air temperature instead. How TAS is estimated walks through the method; the site explorer shows each site's data and fits.
