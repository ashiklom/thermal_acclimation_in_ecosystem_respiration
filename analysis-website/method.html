How TAS is estimated

From half-hourly nighttime CO₂ fluxes to one number per site: the thermal acclimation sensitivity, total and direct.

1. Nighttime respiration and its temperature response

At night, photosynthesis stops and the net CO₂ flux a flux tower measures (net ecosystem exchange, NEE) is ecosystem respiration. Only good nighttime records are used: enough turbulence for the eddy-covariance method to hold (friction velocity above the site's threshold), and plausible values.

The growing season of every site and year is cut into consecutive two-week windows (from the season's start to its end, defined from the seasonal course of NEE). A window is used where it has at least 100 nighttime records over all years and its mean temperature is within the range the respiration model is reliable in. Within a window and year, respiration is modelled as a function of temperature T:

Total model:   R = C0 · exp(α T + β T²)
Direct model:   R = (C0 + k2 · NEEday) · SWC / (Hs + SWC) · exp(α T + β T²)

The direct model adds the two other main controls on respiration that change from year to year along with temperature: the supply of recent photosynthate, indexed by the day's mean daytime net CO₂ uptake (NEEday), and soil moisture (SWC, a saturating response; where a site has no usable soil-moisture record, the ERA5 reanalysis' soil water stands in). Both models are fitted in a Bayesian framework: first pooled over all years of a window (which provides the priors), then for each year, widening the window a few days at a time where a year has too few records.

2. Reference respiration and its ratio across years

Each window has reference conditions: its temperature, NEEday and soil moisture averaged over all years. Evaluating each year's fitted model at the window's reference conditions gives that year's reference respiration, ERref: how much the ecosystem would respire at the same, typical conditions. Differences in ERref between years are therefore not the short-term temperature response — that is what the curve describes — but a shift of the whole curve.

ln ratiowindow, year = ln( ERref, year / ERref, control year )

The control year is one year of the site with good coverage; it only sets the zero of the ratio and does not affect TAS. Extreme ratios (beyond three interquartile ranges and |ln ratio| > 1.5) are discarded.

3. TAS: how the curve shifts with warmer conditions

A window is warmer in some years than in others. If respiration acclimates, the curve of a warmer year sits lower (down-regulation); if it is enhanced, higher. TAS is the slope of the ln ratios on the window-year mean temperatures Twindow, year, with a separate intercept for each window (so that only the differences between years within a window count, not the seasonal course) and errors correlated between successive years (AR(1)), fitted by generalized least squares:

ln ratiow, y = aw + TAS · Tw, y + εw, y,   corr(εw, y, εw, y+1) = ρ

TAS is in units of ln(respiration) per °C: TAS = −0.03 means about 3 % less respiration at the same conditions for each 1 °C a window was warmer. Fitted with the total model it is the total TAS; with the direct model, the direct TAS.

4. Total versus direct TAS, simulated

Warmer periods also change respiration indirectly: for example, a warm spell may come with more photosynthesis and so more fresh substrate for roots and microbes. The total model, with temperature only, takes up these indirect effects into the shift of the curve; the direct model holds substrate supply at its reference value and so removes them.
